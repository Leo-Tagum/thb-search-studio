## Who has to sign, and who else is affected?

The person who holds title, or who has legal authority to sell for the owner, has to sign. Co-owners, heirs, anyone living in the home and others may also be affected, so find out who holds title first.

The house is full. A parent or relative may still live there. The family has not settled who gets to decide. Before cleanout and before price, the first question I ask is who actually holds title.

### Start with who holds title

Title can sit with an individual, a trust, an estate or an LLC. Each one changes who has to sign and who has to agree. You may not know which one applies yet. That is fine. It is the first thing to find out.

### Then ask who has the authority to sell

The person who holds title, or who has the legal authority to sell for the owner, has to sign. Depending on the situation, that could be an owner, a trustee, an executor, an administrator, a surviving spouse, or several heirs agreeing together. Which one applies to your family depends on the documents and the facts, so do not read that list as a rule for your house.

Holding the keys is not the same as having the authority to sign. When the person living in the house is not the person with authority, the sale can stall. If the house was inherited, [find out first whether you can legally sell it](https://www.twinhomebuyer.com/inherited-property-bay-area-legal-authority-to-sell/). A trust, a will, a probate case or several heirs can all change the process.

### Then look at who else is affected

A packed or hoarded house can be tied to family. The seller may be an adult child, an heir or a relative. The person living there may be reluctant to leave, unwell, or not ready. This is someone's home and a family's decision, so go slowly, listen, and keep that person at the center of the plan.

Here is who else can be affected, and why each one matters.

Who | Why they matter
The person living in the home | Their health, wishes and timing shape what is realistic.
Co-owners and heirs | If more than one person has a say, they may all need to agree before anyone can move ahead.
A tenant or other occupant | Anyone else living there affects access, timing and what you can promise a buyer.
Neighbors | If a neighbor has raised concerns, a local office may already know about the property. Ask whether any notice exists before you choose a route.
A professional organizer or cleanout crew | They can help the family sort, keep and clear, and they need clear direction from whoever has authority.

If you are handling a parent's home, [this guide on selling a house full of belongings](https://www.twinhomebuyer.com/sell-bay-area-house-full-of-belongings/) speaks to that situation directly.

[callout] Settle who holds title and who can sign before you pay for a cleanout or talk price with anyone. Spending first can commit you to a plan the right person did not agree to.

[note] Confirm with a real estate attorney or a title company who holds title, who has authority to sell, and who must sign. This guide does not state probate or trust rules.
